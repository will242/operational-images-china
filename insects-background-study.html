Introduction

After 1949, China’s new socialist government set out to transform agriculture with ambitious campaigns. Larger harvests were expected to feed the entire nation and demonstrate the superiority of socialism. Among their foremost tasks was the eradication of crop pests, which had long damaged production. In 1956, a national agricultural programme initiated by Mao Zedong called for the near-elimination of the most destructive crop pests and diseases within seven to twelve years.

Yet pesticides, equipment, and trained personnel were scarce, and many rural residents had little formal schooling. Under these conditions, the state mobilized images as weapons in its war on insects. Scientists, technicians, filmmakers, artists, and peasants themselves were called upon to produce wall charts, illustrated manuals, and science films that taught peasants across the country to identify and destroy pests. This exhibition traces that visual war. Through images made to guide the work of extermination, it explores the entanglement of visuality, agricultural practice, and socialist politics.

Chapter 01

Seeing the Enemy

The Bazha Deity

To wage war on insects, the state first had to train people to see them through its own eyes, as targets for elimination. The task was all the more urgent because many peasants still regarded infestations as natural calamities that could be averted only through appeals to heaven. This woodblock print, made around the fall of the Qing, shows offerings that villagers laid before the Bazha deity in the hope that he would shield their crops from locust plagues and other disasters. The print writes the deity’s name with the character for grasshopper, binding the god to the very insects he was meant to repel. The socialist campaigns sought to eradicate precisely this reliance on ritual.

The Scientific Gaze

In the early campaigns, pest atlases had yet to be produced systematically. Many locally disseminated images therefore borrowed from Chinese and foreign entomological illustration, repurposing images made for scientific research as instruments of extermination. Zhou Yao’s mole cricket drawing exemplifies this practice.

Trained in Italy as an insect taxonomist, Zhou republished his own research drawings as pest illustrations to serve peasants. In his own account, he stressed that his illustrations pursued scientific accuracy while drawing deeply on the Six Principles of the Southern Qi painter and theorist Xie He, uniting close observation with the pursuit of lifelike form. The long antennae, finely veined wings, and powerful digging legs lend the insect both anatomical clarity and aesthetic refinement.

The “Wikipedia” Plate

For all its precision, the single dorsal view of the research illustration was ill suited to the practical work of pest control. As instructional images became more targeted, a new, operational style took shape. This plate from a collectively compiled forestry atlas sets the poplar clearwing moth’s egg, larva, pupa, and adults beside a damaged trunk, much as a Wikipedia entry today gathers related information on a single page. Its numbered details could be consulted separately or read together, helping forestry workers connect damage on a tree’s surface with the insect hidden inside and guiding their efforts to destroy it. This “database” style would become a dominant visual mode in pest-control publications.

Seeing Underground

By the 1960s, yet another way of picturing pests had emerged. Wu Xiaoqing’s pest-identification booklet renders the mole cricket in a manner entirely unlike Zhou Yao’s. An agricultural technician with years of grassroots experience, Wu knew that peasants often saw damaged crops but could not find the insects responsible. Her drawing exposes the cricket’s underground passages, making a hidden pest easier to locate and kill.

The cutaway also gathers eggs, nymphs, and adults into a single view. This arrangement recalls a long tradition of evolutionary iconography in scientific illustration. The German naturalist Ernst Haeckel’s embryological plates offer a classic example, aligning successive stages of development across species to illustrate the unity and continuity of life. Wu’s diagram turns this convention to opposite ends. Rather than affirming continuity, it breaks the life cycle into discrete stages, each a target for killing.

Chapter 02

Science in Peasants’ Hands

Problems of Identification

What distinguished socialist China’s campaign from contemporaneous pest-control efforts in the West was its emphasis on the practical expertise of peasants, the so-called “native experts” (tu zhuanjia). Under the banner of Mao’s On Practice, many peasants were mobilized as researchers and practitioners of pest control. They were encouraged to believe that, even without formal scientific training, practice would enable them to understand insects better than the lordly, foreign-trained experts denounced as bourgeois. A large body of instructional images showed them how to investigate and kill pests on their own.

Beyond ideology, this emphasis on peasant initiative also had a practical basis, since scientists’ images did not always serve their purpose in the field. This chart, distributed to peasants in Tong County, Beijing, in 1975, asks readers to distinguish eighteen kinds of white grub by the shape of the anal opening and the bristles around it. Such differences mattered because some species damaged crops while others were harmless. For peasants working without magnification, however, such minute distinctions were difficult to discern. This gap between expert knowledge and mass practice shaped pest campaigns throughout the Mao era.

Collecting Insects

After 1949, a new genre of instructional images taught ordinary peasants to conduct research of their own. In How to Study Insects, the entomologist Qin Junde used plain language and simple diagrams to explain how to collect insects in the field and study them. His diagrams show, for instance, how to capture insects with a net, how to draw small insects out of soil or leaf litter with a heated separator, and how to dispatch them in a killing bottle and prepare them as specimens. Published in 1956 for peasants and technicians, the manual carried the procedures of entomology beyond the walls of specialist institutions.

Trapping Insects at Night

Another diagram from the same manual shows a lamp illuminating a sheet hung between two trees. Insects drawn to the light could be examined and collected against its pale surface. With nothing more than a sheet and a lamp, peasants could trap pests that were active at night.

The Nine-Grid Drawing Device

In a manual published in 1959, peasants were taught to draw insects without microscopes or other advanced instruments, using a home-grown nine-grid drawing device. A fixed viewing tube directs the eye towards a glass grid above the specimen, and the draughtsperson transfers what appears in each square to the corresponding square of a grid on paper. First introduced by the biological illustrator Feng Chengru, the apparatus allowed an insect’s proportions to be recorded faithfully. Accuracy depended on attentive looking and the coordination of eye and hand.

A Peasant’s Eye

The socialist period produced many “mud-legged” (ni tuizi) peasant entomologists, who began to produce their own images. One such figure was Luo Heshan, a forest ranger in Hubei who spent years investigating the insects that damaged embankment willows. With little formal schooling, he turned a storeroom into a laboratory and reused lamp covers and medicine vials as equipment. Mao’s On Practice sustained his belief in learning through experiment. He gave unfamiliar larvae working names such as “Big Meat Bug No. 1” and “No. 2.” He also devised a way to kill wood-boring larvae by plugging their tunnels with mud.

After Luo’s pest control research acquired promotional value, scientific collaborators helped translate his findings into published diagrams. This plate, one such diagram, sets the life stages of the longhorn beetle that Luo observed beside its tunnels in willow wood, carrying knowledge born of local observation into wider circulation and use.

Chapter 03

Insects Against Insects

Inside the Host Egg

Biological control, which turns natural enemies against pests, was another central strategy of pest control in socialist China. Its rise was driven chiefly by the need to reduce dependence on costly pesticides, though the method also suited the period’s political ideal of self-reliance. Images produced in large numbers helped peasants grasp the hidden relationships between pests and their natural enemies and learn to harness them.

This page shows how Trichogramma, a minute parasitic wasp, lays its eggs inside those of the rice leaffolder, destroying the pest before it can hatch and feed on rice. The photomicrographs follow the young wasp as it feeds, develops, and emerges through the shell, revealing to peasants a killing between insects that would otherwise escape the naked eye.

Breeding Trichogramma

Biological control depended on rearing beneficial insects in vast numbers. This manual taught peasants how to breed Trichogramma, a process that required a steady supply of other insects’ eggs. Its photographs show workers attaching rice moth or eri silkworm eggs to paper strips and then introducing wasps to parasitize them, multiplying the wasps indoors before their release into the fields. The accompanying text specifies materials, quantities, and ratios so that readers could reproduce the procedure.

Surveying the Rice Fields

After rearing came release, as the wasps were set loose in the fields to parasitize the leaffolder. The page demonstrates how to survey the rice fields beforehand, estimating leaffolder moth numbers from sweep-net catches to determine how many wasps were needed.

Releasing the Wasps

The album also presents two methods of release, one using glass bottles and the other bamboo tubes.

Bugs at War on Screen

The state also invested in science and education films, whose moving images could demonstrate biological control more vividly than the printed page. In The Jewel Wasp and the Red Bollworm, macro cinematography brings a pteromalid wasp’s attack on the red bollworm, a cotton pest, into view. The narration casts the wasp as a socialist ally and the bollworm as a bourgeois class enemy. But the images show the wasp greedily parasitizing its enemy, scattering its eggs across the body that will nourish its young. The insects’ own lives thus pull image and narration apart, opening the film to more than one reading.

No Investigation, No Right to Speak

Mao’s campaigns against insects never achieved the results they had envisioned. By the mid-1970s, the state had acknowledged their ecological costs and turned toward integrated control. The instructional images these campaigns generated offer a distinctive lens on their workings. The images mobilized peasants to identify, observe, study, and kill pests, and their practical uses were intricately bound up with socialist politics.

Like the images that precede it, this final image, from a 1973 manual, exemplifies that entanglement. It instructs fruit growers when and how to inspect apple trees for pests, while the text beside it takes as its guiding principle Mao’s dictum “No investigation, no right to speak.” Mao first formulated it in “Oppose Book Worship” (1930) to counter tendencies within the Party to follow the Comintern blindly and to treat its resolutions and Soviet experience as sacred. Here the dictum instead affirms peasants’ own practice as the foundation of the socialist war on insects.
