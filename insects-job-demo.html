Introduction

After 1949, China’s new socialist government set out to transform agriculture with ambitious campaigns. Larger harvests were expected to feed the entire nation and demonstrate the superiority of socialism. Among their foremost tasks was the eradication of crop pests, which had long damaged production. In 1956, a national agricultural programme initiated by Mao Zedong called for the near-elimination of the most destructive crop pests and diseases within seven to twelve years.

Yet pesticides, equipment, and trained personnel were scarce, and many rural residents had little formal schooling. Under these conditions, the state mobilized images as weapons in its war on insects. Scientists, technicians, filmmakers, artists, and peasants themselves were called upon to produce wall charts, illustrated manuals, and science films that taught peasants across the country to identify and destroy pests. This exhibition traces that visual war. Through images made to guide the work of extermination, it explores the entanglement of visuality, agricultural practice, and socialist politics.

Chapter 01

Seeing the Enemy

The Ant Deity

To wage war on insects, the state first had to train people to see them through a scientific lens. The task was all the more urgent because many peasants still regarded infestations as natural calamities that could be averted only through appeals to heaven. This woodblock print, made around the fall of the Qing, depicts offerings to a deity invoked to protect crops from insects and other calamities. Its inscription names the figure 螞蟻神 (“Ant Deity”). Socialist pest-control campaigns sought to replace such ritual appeals with scientific methods.

The Scientific Gaze

In the early campaigns, pest atlases had yet to be produced systematically. Many locally disseminated images therefore repurposed scientific illustrations for pest control. Zhou Yao’s drawing of a mole cricket exemplifies this practice. Trained in Italy as an insect taxonomist, Zhou pursued scientific accuracy while drawing on the aesthetics of Chinese painting, uniting close observation with lifelike form. The long antennae, finely veined wings, and powerful digging legs lend the insect both anatomical clarity and aesthetic refinement.

Seeing Underground

By contrast, Wu Xiaoqing’s instructional manual depicts the mole cricket in its underground habitat. From her years as an agricultural technician, Wu knew that many peasants were puzzled by a pest that damaged their crops yet seemed to disappear into the soil.

Her cutaway shows the insect’s burrows and stages of development, while a circular inset magnifies its digging claws. The image reveals the habits of this hidden pest so that peasants could find and kill it.

The “Wikipedia” Plate

This plate presents another style of pest-control instruction. Much as a Wikipedia entry gathers related information on a single page, it places the poplar clearwing moth’s egg, larva, pupa, and adults beside a damaged trunk. This arrangement helps forestry workers connect damage visible on the tree’s surface with the insect concealed inside. Such encyclopedic illustrations became a common format in pest-control publications.

Chapter 02

Science in Peasants’ Hands

Problems of Identification

Yet when these images reached the fields, they did not always work as intended. Published in 1975, this chart asks plant-protection workers to distinguish eighteen kinds of white grub, including crop pests and beneficial species, by the shape of the anal opening and the surrounding bristles.

Without magnification or sufficient training, such minute differences were difficult for peasants to discern in the field. This gap between expert knowledge and mass practice remained a central problem in pest-control campaigns.

Collecting Insects

One response to this difficulty was to encourage peasants to investigate pests themselves, reflecting the Mao-era commitment to combining scientific research with mass participation. In How to Study Insects (1956), the entomologist Qin Junde used plain language and simple diagrams to teach readers how to collect and study insects. The illustrations show how to capture insects with a net or extract insects from soil and leaf litter with a heated separator. The manual carried the procedures of entomology beyond specialist institutions.

Trapping Insects at Night

Another diagram from the same manual shows a lamp illuminating a sheet hung between two trees. Insects drawn to the light could be examined and collected against its pale surface. With nothing more than a sheet and a lamp, peasants could trap pests that were active at night.

The Nine-Grid Drawing Device

In a 1959 manual, the biological illustrator Feng Chengru introduced a nine-grid drawing apparatus that allowed specimens to be drawn without a microscope. The specimen sits beneath a gridded glass plate; viewed through a fixed tube, its details are copied square by square onto gridded paper. The apparatus allowed the specimen’s proportions to be recorded faithfully. Its accuracy still depended on the coordination of eye and hand.

A Peasant’s Eye

As instructional images circulated alongside mass political campaigns, peasants increasingly took part in the production of scientific knowledge. A prominent example was Luo Heshan, a “mud-legged” (ni tuizi) peasant entomologist and embankment caretaker in Hubei who spent years studying insects that damaged willow groves. With little formal schooling, he turned a storeroom into a makeshift laboratory and gave unfamiliar larvae working names such as “Big Meat Bug No. 1.” As his investigations gained wider recognition, scientific collaborators helped prepare his findings for publication. This plate places the longhorn beetle’s life stages beside its tunnels in willow wood, translating local observations into a form that could circulate more widely.

Chapter 03

Insects Against Insects

Inside the Host Egg

Biological control, which turns natural enemies against pests, was another central strategy in socialist China. It offered a way to reduce dependence on costly pesticides and aligned with the period’s political ideal of self-reliance. This page shows how Trichogramma, a minute parasitic wasp, lays its eggs inside the rice leaffolder’s eggs, preventing the pest from hatching. The photomicrographs follow the wasp’s development within the host egg until it emerges through the shell, revealing a form of insect-on-insect killing otherwise invisible to the naked eye.

Breeding Trichogramma

Biological control depended on rearing beneficial insects in vast numbers. This manual taught peasants how to breed Trichogramma, a process that required a steady supply of other insects’ eggs. Its photographs show workers attaching rice moth or eri silkworm eggs to paper strips and then introducing wasps to parasitize them, multiplying the wasps indoors before their release into the fields. The accompanying text specifies materials, quantities, and ratios so that readers could reproduce the procedure.

Surveying the Rice Fields

After rearing came release, as the wasps were set loose in the fields to parasitize the leaffolder. The page demonstrates how to survey the rice fields beforehand, using rice leaffolder moth counts to determine how many wasps were needed.

Releasing the Wasps

The album also presents two methods of release, one using glass bottles and the other bamboo tubes.

Bugs at War on Screen

Science and education films could demonstrate biological control more vividly than the printed page. In The Jewel Wasp and the Red Bollworm, microcinematography brings a pteromalid wasp’s attack on the pink bollworm, a cotton pest, into view. The narration casts the wasp as a socialist ally and the bollworm as a bourgeois class enemy. Yet the wasp repeatedly pierces its host with its ovipositor and deposits its eggs. The insects’ worldmaking thus sets the image at odds with the political narration, opening the film to multiple meanings.

No Investigation, No Right to Speak

The socialist campaigns against insects did not achieve the results their planners had envisioned. Yet the instructional images they produced offer a distinctive perspective on how those campaigns worked. They mobilized peasants to study and kill pests, making visual instruction an integral part of socialist agricultural practice.

Like the images before it, this final illustration, from a 1973 manual, exemplifies the close relationship between practical instruction and socialist politics. It shows fruit growers how to inspect apple trees for pests, while the accompanying text invokes Mao’s dictum, “No investigation, no right to speak.” In his 1930 essay “Oppose Book Worship,” Mao used the phrase to challenge unquestioning adherence to external directives, including those of the Comintern. Here it lends political authority to peasants’ own investigation, placing their practical knowledge at the center of the socialist war on insects.
